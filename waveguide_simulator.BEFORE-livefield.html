Space survivability

    
Can this part actually survive in orbit?

    
Low loss is necessary but not sufficient — before an RF part flies, two vacuum-specific failure modes decide whether it works: multipaction (an electron avalanche that detonates the signal at high power) and overheating (in vacuum a wall can only shed absorbed power by radiation). These first-order screens flag both. They're the go/no-go questions no generic EM tool asks.


    
    
1 · Multipaction screening

    
At high power in hard vacuum, electrons knocked off the wall can multiply in resonance with the RF field and avalanche — destroying the signal. Susceptibility scales with the frequency×gap product and the peak gap voltage.

    

      

        

          

        

          

      

      

      
Gap voltage from TE₁₀ power, $V \approx b\sqrt{4\eta_0 P /\,(a b \sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2})}$ (Pozar [6]); multipaction risk assessed against the frequency×gap susceptibility of ECSS-E-ST-20-01C [32].

    


    
    
2 · Power handling & heating in vacuum

    
The wall absorbs a little of the signal as heat. In air that convects away; in vacuum the wall can only radiate it. This finds the steady-state wall temperature and the maximum power before the coating degrades.

    

      

        

          

        

          

        

          

        

          

        

          

        

          

        

          

      

      

      
Absorbed power from the analytical wall loss; radiative steady state $P_{\text{abs}} = \varepsilon\,\sigma_{SB} A (T^4 - T_{\text{env}}^4)$ (Stefan–Boltzmann [34]). Safe-temperature limit from Ti₃C₂Tₓ thermal stability — oxidation onset ≈ 350 °C in air, structurally stable to ≈ 600 °C in vacuum/inert [33].

    


    
Both are first-order screens, not qualification. A quantitative multipaction verdict needs the wall's secondary-electron-yield curve and a full ECSS analysis [32] — and MXene's SEY is essentially unmeasured, which is itself an open research question (does a MXene surface raise or lower the multipaction threshold vs metal?). The thermal model is a lumped radiative balance; real hardware also conducts heat to its mounts, which helps. Treat these as design-guidance flags, then verify by test.

Everything the simulator computes comes from one chain of physics: an electromagnetic wave travels down a hollow rectangular pipe, and the imperfectly-conducting walls steal a little energy from it on every bounce. Here is the full derivation, in order.

    

      Hand this derivation to your advisor →
      
    


    
1
The wave and its mode

    
Inside a rectangular guide of internal width $a$ and height $b$ (with $a>b$), the lowest-order wave pattern that can travel is the TE10 mode — the standard operating mode for satellite waveguides. Maxwell's equations, solved for this geometry, give every result below (Pozar, Microwave Engineering [6]).


    
ε
μ
Material properties: permittivity ε and permeability μ

    
Every material enters Maxwell's equations through three numbers: conductivity $\sigma$, permittivity (its electric response) and permeability (its magnetic response). The last two are quoted relative to free space [6]:

    
$$ \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon_r, \qquad \mu = \mu_0\,\mu_r $$

    
with $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m and $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m. Permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ sets how the material stores electric energy; permeability $\mu_r$ how it stores magnetic energy. Together they fix the wave impedance and how fast/short the wave is inside the material:

    
$$ \eta = \sqrt{\tfrac{\mu}{\varepsilon}} = \eta_0\sqrt{\tfrac{\mu_r}{\varepsilon_r}}, \qquad \beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon} = \frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{\varepsilon_r\,\mu_r} $$

    
For a conductor the free-electron response dominates and is folded into a complex permittivity:

    
$$ \varepsilon_c = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r - j\,\frac{\sigma}{\omega} $$

    
Magnetic note. MXene, copper, aluminium and wood are all essentially non-magnetic, so $\mu_r \approx 1$ — magnetically they behave like free space, and their loss is dielectric/ohmic rather than magnetic. A material only gets $\mu_r \neq 1$ if it contains ferromagnetic/ferrite content. You can set $\varepsilon_r$ and $\mu_r$ for each layer in the Materials tab; they flow into the skin depth, wave impedance and reflection equations that follow.


    
2
Cutoff frequency — can the wave travel?

    
A waveguide is a high-pass filter: below a cutoff frequency $f_c$, the wave cannot propagate at all. For TE10 it depends only on the width $a$ [6]:

    
$$ f_c = \frac{c}{2a} $$

    
where $c$ is the speed of light. This is why each band uses a different guide size — wider guides for lower frequencies.


    
3
Skin depth — how deep the current flows

    
At microwave frequencies the wall current crowds into an extremely thin surface layer. Its characteristic depth is the skin depth $\delta$ [6]:

    
$$ \delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f \mu_0 \mu_r \sigma}} $$

    
$f$ = frequency, $\mu_0\mu_r$ = permeability ($\mu_r\approx1$ for these non-magnetic materials), $\sigma$ = conductivity. This is the single most important quantity in the whole MXene story: because MXene conducts ~58× worse than copper, and skin depth scales as $1/\sqrt{\sigma}$, its skin depth is about $\sqrt{58}\approx8\times$ larger (~5 µm vs ~0.66 µm at 10 GHz). A coating thinner than the skin depth lets the wave "leak" straight through it. (A magnetic material with $\mu_r>1$ has a thinner skin depth and therefore higher wall loss — that is how permeability enters.)


    
4
Surface resistance — how lossy the wall is

    
The energy lost per bounce is set by the wall's surface resistance $R_s$. For a thick wall [6]:

    
$$ R_s = \frac{1}{\sigma \delta} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi f \mu_0}{\sigma}} $$

    
But a thin coating on a non-conducting substrate (MXene on wood) is not a thick wall. If the coating thickness $t$ is comparable to or smaller than $\delta$, the effective resistance rises sharply (thin-film surface impedance [6]):

    
$$ R_s^{\text{eff}} = \frac{R_s}{\tanh\!\left(t/\delta\right)} $$

    
When $t \gg \delta$, $\tanh \to 1$ and the coating behaves like solid metal. When $t \ll \delta$, $R_s^{\text{eff}}$ blows up — a useless wall. As a rule of thumb the coating needs $t \gtrsim 3\delta$; that is exactly the "coating ÷ skin depth" indicator on the simulator, and why stacking ~15–20 coats matters.


    
5
Attenuation — putting it together

    
The TE10 conductor attenuation (Pozar, Microwave Engineering [6]) gives how fast the signal decays along the guide, in nepers per metre:

    
$$ \alpha_c = \frac{R_s^{\text{eff}}}{b\,\eta_0\sqrt{1-(f_c/f)^2}}\left[\,1 + \frac{2b}{a}\left(\frac{f_c}{f}\right)^{2}\right] \;\text{[Np/m]} $$

    
where $\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} \approx 376.7\ \Omega$ is the impedance of free space. Converting to decibels:

    
$$ \alpha_{\text{dB/m}} = 8.686 \times \alpha_c $$

    
This $\alpha_{\text{dB/m}}$ is the curve plotted on the simulator's first chart.

    
Multi-layer coatings (Full-wave tab). When several coating layers are stacked on the block (e.g. inner MXene, then graphene), the wall is a cascade of thin transmission-line sections. Its effective inner surface impedance is found from the layer transfer (ABCD) matrix [6] terminated by the substrate, $Z_{\text{in}} = (A Z_L + B)/(C Z_L + D)$; then $R_s^{\text{eff}} = \mathrm{Re}(Z_{\text{in}})$ feeds the same $\alpha_c$ above (Layer 1 = innermost, wave-facing). The innermost layer dominates when it is thicker than a few skin depths; a thin inner layer lets the wave reach the layers behind it. For the openEMS run, an effective conductivity $\sigma_{\text{eff}} = \pi f \mu_0 / R_s^{\text{eff}\,2}$ reproduces the stack as a single impedance-boundary wall (a literal µm-thin metal stack can't be resolved at FDTD mesh sizes).


    
On frequency dependence. The conductivity $\sigma$ is treated as constant across 8–27 GHz, which is well-justified for a metallic conductor like MXene. The frequency effect that actually matters enters automatically through $\delta(f)$ and $R_s(f)$. (The complex-permittivity data in MXene papers describes wave absorbers — the opposite goal of a low-loss waveguide.)


    
P
Microwave interaction: absorption, penetration depth & heating

    
How strongly a material couples to a microwave field is captured by its complex permittivity and permeability — the real parts store energy, the imaginary parts $\varepsilon_r''$ and $\mu_r''$ dissipate it as heat [11]:

    
$$ \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r' - j\varepsilon_r''), \qquad \mu = \mu_0(\mu_r' - j\mu_r'') $$

    
The ratio of loss to storage is the loss tangent. Including the ohmic (conduction) contribution, the effective loss tangent is [6][11]:

    
$$ \tan\delta = \frac{\varepsilon_r''}{\varepsilon_r'}, \qquad \tan\delta_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\sigma + \omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r''}{\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r'} $$

    
The power absorbed per unit volume has three channels — conduction/Joule heating, dielectric loss, and magnetic loss [11]:

    
$$ P = \tfrac12\,\sigma|E|^2 \;+\; \pi f\,\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r''|E|^2 \;+\; \pi f\,\mu_0\mu_r''|H|^2 $$

    
The penetration depth $D_p$ is how far the field reaches into the material before its amplitude falls to $1/e$ [11]:

    
$$ D_p = \left[\frac{\omega^2\varepsilon'\mu'}{2}\left(\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\sigma+\omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r''}{\omega\varepsilon'}\right)^{\!2}}-1\right)\right]^{-1/2} $$

    
with $\varepsilon' = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r'$ and $\mu' = \mu_0\mu_r'$. For a good conductor ($\sigma \gg \omega\varepsilon'$) this collapses to the skin depth $\delta$ of step 3 — so $D_p$ is simply its general form. Finally, the rate a lossy material heats under the field [11]:

    
$$ \frac{\Delta T}{\Delta t} = \frac{2\pi f\,\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r''|E|^2}{\rho\,C_p} $$

    
where $\rho$ is density and $C_p$ the specific heat. These four quantities are reported on the Simulator cards (loss tangent, penetration depth, dominant loss mechanism).

    
How this enters the waveguide numbers. The dielectric loss is folded into an effective conductivity $\sigma_{\text{eff}} = \sigma + \omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r''$, and magnetic loss enters through the complex surface impedance $Z_s = \sqrt{j\omega\mu/\sigma_{\text{eff}}}$ with $\mu = \mu_0(\mu_r'-j\mu_r'')$; its real part $R_s = \mathrm{Re}(Z_s)$ replaces the surface resistance of step 4. So $\varepsilon_r''$ and $\mu_r''$ change every downstream loss number. These absorption/penetration/heating relations follow the microwave–matter framework of Elkaffas et al., NPG Asia Materials (2026) [11]. For a good conductor like MXene the $\sigma$ term dominates and $\mu_r''=0$; the magnetic term matters only for ferrite/magnetic composites.


    
6
Weight model

    
A section is four thin walls. With perimeter $P = 2(a+b)$, wall thickness $t_{\text{wall}}$, length $L$ and density $\rho$ (densities [5][15][16]):

    
$$ m = P \cdot t_{\text{wall}} \cdot L \cdot \rho $$

    
For coated basswood, the wood core and the (micron-thin) coating are summed. The weight win comes from replacing dense metal ($\rho_{\text{Cu}}\approx 8960$) with light basswood ($\rho\approx 415$ kg/m³).


    
7
Cost model — why launch dominates

    
The real cost of a part in orbit is what you pay to build it plus what you pay to launch its mass (launch cost [9]):

    
$$ C_{\text{total}} = C_{\text{material}} + m \cdot C_{\text{launch}} $$

    
With launch around \$7,000/kg, the launch term usually dwarfs the material term. That is the whole argument for lightweight waveguides: a cheap-but-heavy copper guide can cost far more to fly than an expensive-but-light coated-wood one.

Horn antenna — full-wave model (openEMS)

    
A horn is an open, radiating structure, so unlike the closed waveguide it is solved with FDTD using open (PML) boundaries and a near-field-to-far-field (NF2FF) transform. A WR-waveguide feed launches the TE10 mode into a flared aperture. The relations below are the antenna figures of merit reported on the Horn tab — standard antenna theory (Balanis [25]), validated against a rated 15 dBi standard-gain horn and matched to the metric set of Sarpanah Sourkouhi et al. [26].


    
Aperture, directivity & gain

    
Feed cutoff and the free-space wavelength at the operating frequency $f_0$ [6]:

    
$$ f_c = \frac{c}{2a}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{c}{f_0} $$

    
The far-field radiation intensity $U$ and directivity $D$ follow from the radiated field $E_{\text{ff}}(\theta,\phi)$ — this is exactly what the NF2FF transform computes [10][25]:

    
$$ U(\theta,\phi) = \frac{r^2}{2\eta_0}\,|E_{\text{ff}}(\theta,\phi)|^2, \qquad D(\theta,\phi) = \frac{4\pi\,U(\theta,\phi)}{P_{\text{rad}}}, \qquad D_{\max}=\max_{\theta,\phi} D(\theta,\phi) $$

    
For an inner aperture of size $A_x \times A_y$, the ideal (uniform-illumination) directivity and the aperture efficiency are [25]:

    
$$ D_{\text{ap}} = \frac{4\pi A_x A_y}{\lambda^2}, \qquad e_{\text{ap}} = \frac{D_{\max}}{D_{\text{ap}}} $$


    
Beam shape

    
From the principal-plane patterns the tool extracts the half-power beamwidth (the −3 dB angular width around boresight), the first sidelobe level (peak-to-first-lobe ratio) and the front-to-back ratio [25]:

    
$$ \text{HPBW} = \theta_{-3\,\text{dB}}^{\,+} - \theta_{-3\,\text{dB}}^{\,-}, \qquad \text{F/B} = G(0^\circ) - G(180^\circ) $$

    
Quick pyramidal-horn cross-checks: $\text{HPBW}_E \approx 56\,\lambda/A_y$ and $\text{HPBW}_H \approx 67\,\lambda/A_x$ (degrees). The co-/cross-polarization patterns come from the two far-field components — E-plane: co $=|E_\theta|$, cross $=|E_\phi|$; H-plane: co $=|E_\phi|$, cross $=|E_\theta|$.


    
Input match: S11, VSWR, impedance

    
At the feed port the reflection coefficient $\Gamma$ (= $S_{11}$) is the ratio of reflected to incident wave; from it come the return loss, its phase, the VSWR and the input impedance [6][25]:

    
$$ \Gamma = S_{11} = \frac{V_{\text{ref}}}{V_{\text{inc}}}, \quad S_{11}[\text{dB}]=20\log_{10}|\Gamma|, \quad \text{VSWR}=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}, \quad Z_{\text{in}}=\frac{V}{I} $$

    
The impedance bandwidth is the frequency span where $|S_{11}| \lt -10$ dB (i.e. $|\Gamma| \lt 0.316$). The mismatch efficiency is the fraction of incident power the antenna accepts:

    
$$ e_{\text{mis}} = 1 - |\Gamma|^2 $$


    
Radiation efficiency & realized gain

    
The radiation efficiency is radiated over accepted power; the realized gain folds in both wall loss and the input mismatch [25]:

    
$$ e_{\text{rad}} = \frac{P_{\text{rad}}}{P_{\text{acc}}}, \qquad G_{\text{realized}} = e_{\text{rad}}\,\bigl(1-|\Gamma|^2\bigr)\,D_{\max} $$


    
Wall (coating) loss — surface-impedance method

    
FDTD cannot mesh a good conductor's skin depth (≪ the cell size), so conductor loss on the horn walls is obtained by the surface-impedance perturbation of the full-wave field: solve once with perfect walls (PEC), then integrate the tangential magnetic field over the walls for each coating conductivity $\sigma$ [6][26]:

    
$$ R_s = \sqrt{\frac{\pi f \mu_0}{\sigma}}, \qquad P_c = \frac{R_s}{2}\oint_{\text{walls}} |H_{\text{tan}}|^2\,dA, \qquad e_{\text{rad}} = \frac{P_{\text{rad}}}{P_{\text{rad}} + P_c} $$

    
The gain penalty of a coating versus ideal metal is $10\log_{10}(e_{\text{rad}})$ dB. Because $P_c \propto 1/\sqrt{\sigma}$, a single PEC solve yields the result for every coating.


    
Method & validity (horn). Solved with openEMS FDTD [10], open PML boundaries and the NF2FF transform for gain and pattern. The openEMS loss magnitude is coarse-mesh approximate — gain, pattern and reflection are the trustworthy outputs; the coating comparison uses the surface-impedance method above (a separate lossy FDTD per coating does not resolve the skin depth, so all coatings come from one solve). Directivity validates against the rated ~15 dBi standard-gain horn (Pasternack PE9856B-15) [27], and the reported quantities mirror Sarpanah Sourkouhi et al., Adv. Mater. Technol. (2026) [26].


    
FF
Radiation from the open end — far-field pattern

    
A waveguide's open end radiates like an aperture antenna. For the TE10 aperture (broad wall $a$, narrow wall $b$) the normalized principal-plane patterns are (Balanis [25]):

    
$$ f_E(\theta) = \frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}\,\operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\tfrac{kb}{2}\sin\theta\right), \qquad f_H(\theta) = \frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}\cdot\frac{\cos\!\left(\tfrac{ka}{2}\sin\theta\right)}{1-\left(\tfrac{ka}{\pi}\sin\theta\right)^2} $$

    
with $k=2\pi/\lambda$. The E-plane ($\phi=90^\circ$) uses the uniform aperture field across the narrow wall $b$; the H-plane ($\phi=0^\circ$) the cosine field across the broad wall $a$. The full-wave openEMS Far-field simulation instead computes the pattern directly from the recorded near fields via the near-field-to-far-field (NF2FF) transform [10] — enclosing the aperture in a Huygens box and applying the equivalence principle — which also yields the directivity $D = 4\pi U_{\max}/P_{\text{rad}}$. Both are overlaid on the Full-wave tab's far-field chart.

    
Full-wave method & the field-view toggle. openEMS is a time-domain solver: it launches a broadband Gaussian pulse and steps Maxwell's equations through time on a 3-D grid (the FDTD method [10][35]). Every frequency-domain result in this tool — S-parameters, far-field, R/T/A — is obtained by a running discrete Fourier transform of that single time-domain run, so one solve covers the whole band at once. The Full-wave and Horn tabs' Field view selector only changes how the field is displayed, not the solver: time-domain is the moving-wave animation, and frequency-domain is the steady-state $|E|$ magnitude at the band-centre frequency from the on-the-fly DFT dump (whose along-guide ripple is the standing-wave pattern set by any residual waveguide-port reflection).


    
ID
Inverse design — Pareto-optimal trade-offs

    
The Inverse-design tab turns the model around: instead of scoring one wall, it evaluates every catalog design and finds the best trade-off between signal loss and a resource cost. Each design's loss is the TE10 attenuation $\alpha_{\text{dB/m}}$ (§5, using the multilayer $R_s^{\text{eff}}$ where coated); its per-metre mass, delivered cost and embodied carbon are

    
$$ m = P\left(t_{\text{sub}}\rho_{\text{sub}} + t_{\text{coat}}\rho_{\text{coat}}\right), \qquad C_{\text{deliv}} = \sum_i m_i p_i \;+\; m\,c_{\text{launch}}, \qquad \text{CO}_2 = \sum_i m_i\,g_i $$

    
where $P=2(a+b)$ is the wall perimeter and $t,\rho,p,g$ are each layer's thickness, density, price and embodied-carbon factor, and $c_{\text{launch}}$ the launch cost per kg. With two objectives (loss and, e.g., mass) a design $\mathbf{x}$ dominates $\mathbf{y}$ when it is no worse on both and strictly better on one:

    
$$ \mathbf{x}\succ\mathbf{y} \iff f_i(\mathbf{x})\le f_i(\mathbf{y})\ \ \forall i \quad\text{and}\quad f_j(\mathbf{x})\lt f_j(\mathbf{y})\ \text{ for some } j $$

    
The Pareto-optimal frontier is the set of non-dominated designs — the curve where loss cannot fall without adding mass/cost/carbon, or vice-versa. The tool returns that frontier plus the lowest-objective design meeting your loss target (Marler & Arora [28]).


    
FIT
Extracting conductivity from a measurement (inverse fit)

    
The Measurement-fit tab inverts the same loss model. Given a measured transmission $S_{21}^{\text{meas}}(f_i)$ over a guide of length $L$, the modelled transmission for a trial conductivity is $S_{21}^{\text{mod}}(f;\sigma) = -\alpha_{\text{dB/m}}(f;\sigma)\,L$. The effective wall conductivity is the least-squares best fit

    
$$ \sigma^\star = \arg\min_{\sigma}\ \sum_i\left[\,S_{21}^{\text{mod}}(f_i;\sigma) - S_{21}^{\text{meas}}(f_i)\,\right]^2 $$

    
solved by a golden-section search on $\log_{10}\sigma$ (Kiefer [31]). The reported confidence range is the span of $\sigma$ for which the RMS residual stays within a tolerance of its minimum. The extraction assumes wall-dominated loss (a matched guide); connector or mismatch loss biases $\sigma^\star$ low.


    
UQ
Uncertainty quantification

    
Several inputs are genuinely uncertain — above all MXene's conductivity, reported anywhere from $\sim\!10^4$ to $2.4\times10^6$ S/m across the literature [1][2]. Quoting a single loss number would be false precision. The honest approach is to treat each uncertain input as a probability distribution, propagate many samples through the model, and report the distribution of the output rather than one value (JCGM 101 Monte-Carlo method [30]).

    
For an output $y=g(\mathbf{x})$ with uncertain inputs $\mathbf{x}\sim p(\mathbf{x})$, draw $N$ samples $\mathbf{x}^{(k)}$ and evaluate:

    
$$ \bar{y} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} g\!\left(\mathbf{x}^{(k)}\right), \qquad \text{90\% coverage interval } \left[\,y_{5\%},\,y_{95\%}\,\right] \text{ from the sample percentiles.} $$

    
Reporting, e.g., "loss $=0.9$ dB/m, 90% CI $[0.4,\,2.1]$" makes the conclusion robust to the input spread. A first-order sensitivity — how much of the output variance each input explains (Sobol indices, or one-at-a-time variation) — then shows which property (here almost always $\sigma$ and the coat count) dominates and is worth pinning down experimentally.

    
How this connects to the tool. The Materials/References tabs already carry the σ ranges, and the analytical engine is instant, so a Monte-Carlo pass over those ranges is cheap to run. The Measurement-fit tab is the empirical counterpart — it replaces an assumed σ with one measured on your own sample, collapsing the largest source of uncertainty.

This tool tests whether lightweight coated materials — flagship MXene-coated basswood — could replace solid metal for satellite waveguides and horn antennas: far lighter, but do they still carry, reflect and radiate microwave signals well? It works three ways: a fast built-in model that runs instantly in your browser, a full-wave solver (openEMS) that runs the real 3-D electromagnetic simulation, and design/characterization tools that pick the optimal material for you and read a real conductivity back from a lab measurement. It runs on macOS or Windows (setup in step 6).


    
1
What a "simulator" actually is

    
A simulator is a calculator with physics behind it: you describe a situation (a wave, a material, a shape) and it predicts what happens, using equations we know are correct, instead of building hardware. That lets you ask "what if the coating were thicker?" or "what about a higher frequency?" and get an answer in seconds. Everything here rests on Maxwell's equations — the laws governing electromagnetic waves. A material enters those equations as just a few numbers: its conductivity (how well it carries current), permittivity, and permeability.


    
2
Two kinds of simulation in this tool

    
The analytical model (Simulator, Compare, Materials tabs) solves the equations with exact formulas — instant, runs in the browser, great for exploring. A full-wave simulation (Full-wave tab, via openEMS) instead chops space into a 3-D grid and steps the fields through time (the FDTD method) — slower, but it produces the real field patterns and animations and validates the fast model. Commercial tools like openEMS performs this full-wave simulation — free and Mac-native.

Run a full-wave simulation

    
In the Full-wave tab, choose band, material, coats and length, and optionally the number of ports (1-port = reflection / S11 only; 2-port = through, S11 + S21) and the mesh density (finer = more accurate but more RAM — the estimated cell count and memory are shown live). Reading the results

    
    
      
  • Insertion loss (dB/m) — how much signal is lost travelling down the guide; lower is better.
    • 
      
  • R / T / A — fractions reflected, transmitted, absorbed; they sum to 100%. For a good waveguide wall you want high reflection (≈99.8% for MXene-coated basswood — like metal).
    • 
      
  • Weight & delivered cost — MXene-coated basswood is ~95% lighter than copper (≈46 vs ≈890 g per metre of X-band guide), and once the ~$7,000/kg launch cost is counted, far cheaper to put in orbit.

Geometry preview — pre-simulation model

        

      

      
    

    

      
A pre-simulation geometry preview (the "model view" or "structure view") shows the object that will be simulated — its shape, wall material, and where the wave is fed in — before you run anything.

      
It is drawn live in your browser with WebGL (Three.js) from the settings you picked (band → guide cross-section, length, coats, ports). Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom; it appears instantly and needs no server. The dimensions and the input/output port positions are correct; it is a schematic (not the solver's FDTD mesh), and because the conductive coating is only microns thick it is shown as a highlighted inner lining, exaggerated so you can see it. It does not compute any fields — it only shows the model. A colour key and the exact dimensions are printed beneath.
